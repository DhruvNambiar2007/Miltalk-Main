For primary and secondary schools · Ages 9–14

What happens in
a Miltalk session?

We start with the ideas behind AI classification, help children train a kit, then put their model to work in a project.

  1. 01 · Learn

    Understand the idea

    Start with a short explanation of how machines learn from examples and sort things into categories.

  2. 02 · Explore

    Teach the kit

    Collect pictures or sounds, train a model and investigate what changes its predictions.

  3. 03 · Apply

    Build a project

    Give the model a practical job, such as controlling a sorting arm. Children can see why the right prediction matters.

What makes Miltalk different?

The lesson continues in a project.

After the theory and kit practice, we introduce a project to keep the session engaging and give children a reason to use what they have learned. They return to the same ideas about examples, categories and predictions as they work towards a goal.

That connection between a classification lesson and a physical task is central to Miltalk. We design the projects to reinforce the concepts through application and support retention after the session.

About the product

A few practical questions.

What is Miltalk?

Miltalk is a set of handheld learning kits that students train with their own examples. They choose two categories, collect data and test the kit’s predictions.

What is the difference between the two kits?

The Vision Kit has a camera and learns from pictures. The Audio Kit has a microphone and learns from sounds.

What age group is Miltalk for?

Miltalk is designed for children aged 9 to 14 in primary and secondary schools. We adapt the explanation and activities to the group.

Does our school have to plan the activities?

No. We plan and lead the session, supply the kits and learning materials, and guide the students. Your school shares its group size, age range and scheduling requirements.

Why do we teach classification?

Classification gives children a clear way to explore AI: collect examples, choose categories and test a prediction. It also helps them understand familiar applications such as spam filters and image recognition.

How do the projects relate to classification?

The model’s predicted category controls what happens next. In the recycling sorter we are developing, a recyclable or non-recyclable prediction determines which way a servo motor turns the sorting arm.

Why add projects after training and testing?

A project keeps the session engaging and gives children a goal to work towards. Applying classification in a physical task lets them revisit the theory, connect a prediction to an action and practise improving their examples. We design this part of the session to reinforce learning and support retention.
